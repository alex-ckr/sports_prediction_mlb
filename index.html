Every game gets one number: how often we think a team would win if this exact game were played over and over. Sixty percent doesn't mean they'll win — it means that in a hundred repeats of tonight, they'd take about sixty.

Kalshi is a market where a contract pays $1 if a team wins. If it costs 57¢, the market is saying that team wins about 57 times out of 100. Our number and the market's number are two estimates of the same thing.

Where they disagree is the only place there's anything to do — and even then, mostly what a disagreement means is that one of the two is wrong.

Three things worth knowing before you act on anything

1. The market is usually right. Kalshi's prices come from many people with real money at stake, all watching the same news. Our model is a set of formulas with about a dozen hand-set numbers. When they disagree, the prior should be that the model is wrong.

2. Buying costs more than the price. You buy at the ask, not the middle, and Kalshi charges a fee. A contract quoted around 56¢ typically needs the team to win about 59 times out of 100 before you make anything. That gap is on every card as break-even.

3. A number built on missing data is not a number. When the feed can't supply starting-pitcher or bullpen quality, the model falls back to records and home field — which is close to a coin flip. Those games are marked and their percentage is hidden rather than shown, because showing it invites trusting it.

Glossary

Our estimate
How often the model thinks this team wins, out of 100 games.
Market price
What a $1 payout contract costs on Kalshi right now, in cents. 57¢ means the market thinks 57 out of 100.
Break-even
How often the team actually has to win for you to come out even after the price and the fee. Always higher than the market price.
Difference
Our estimate minus break-even. Positive means the model thinks the contract is cheap. It does not mean the model is right.
Data quality
How many of the six input groups the data feed actually supplied for this game.
Starter / bullpen quality
FIP — an ERA-like number built only from strikeouts, walks and home runs, which is more stable than ERA. Lower is better; about 4.10 is average.

Are the estimates honest?

If the model says 70% and those picks win 70% of the time, it's calibrated and worth listening to. If they win 55% of the time, it's overconfident and every "edge" it finds is imaginary.
